# I-1 · Cold open

## Slide placeholder (copy into Canva)

**Main point.** The audience *performs* a transitive inference themselves in
fifteen seconds — then I pose the question the whole talk answers: where in the
brain is a relational order stored, and does it survive after you stop thinking?

**Concepts to land.**
- Give only **neighbour** facts about four guests in the room:
  Battiston > Gabrielli > Garlaschelli > Meloni (fastest → slowest).
- Stage a **race** whose matchups are the pairs you were *never* shown —
  Battiston vs Garlaschelli, Gabrielli vs Meloni. Filling the bracket *is* the
  inference. Battiston wins it all; Meloni goes out first (inside joke).
- The order becomes a **tree** — the first, wordless appearance of the hierarchy
  the method recovers later.
- Close on the thesis question: **which relational structures in the human brain
  store an order like this — and do they keep holding it after you stop
  thinking?**
- Optional aside: two of the four (Battiston, Gabrielli) authored the methods
  used later in the talk.

**Figures / visuals.**
- Race bracket — four photos as leaves resolving to a champion, the inferred
  (never-shown) matchup marked.

  add the four headshots in Canva.

  light-slide variant on request.)

**References.** None on-slide. (Battiston cited at I-3; Gabrielli at I-5.)

## Keep honest (content constraints, not styling)

- Show **only the neighbour premises** — never the sorted list, or the inference
  is given away.
- No data, no method, no "brain as a network" yet — this slide is pure hook.
- The bracket is a **race** (pairs non-adjacent to force inference), *not* the
  UPGMA dendrogram (which merges neighbours). Only "order → tree" carries to I-6.

## Open decision (content)

- Cold-open framing: transitive-inference story (recommended) vs the
  method-wonder alternative ("watch a thought become geometry").
