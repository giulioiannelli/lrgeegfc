# Slide 6 — Why it matters: four literature anchors, each → a facet of our work (text-forward)

1. TITLE
Why it matters: from detecting a change to characterizing it

2. MAIN CONCEPT
- Head (the juice): humans infer rich relational structure **from very little data**, where machine learning is data-hungry — few-shot relational reasoning is a **hallmark of human intelligence**. Four ideas from the literature tell us *where to look*, and each maps onto a facet of what we do: **cognitive maps** (relations stored as a multi-scale map → our hierarchical/multiscale lens), **offline consolidation** (built at rest, not at learning → the encoding-vs-inference distinction), **replay** (structure reactivated & reorganized offline → the resting *trace* we look for), and **data-efficiency** (why the human brain's solution is worth reading out). All of it has been established by behaviour and theory — never *read, quantitatively, in the brain's multi-scale network*. That is our move — and the same lens reads a network disease, epilepsy.
- Beat 1 — DATA-EFFICIENCY / the "AI" kick (why humans are worth studying): people infer a whole relational structure from a handful of examples, where modern machine learning needs enormous data (Liu–Behrens 2019, opening line — "rich inferences from little data by generalizing how things relate"). This is a claim about **data**, not reasoning superiority — keep it there (see guardrail). → *our angle:* the human brain solves few-shot relational inference; that solution is what we read out.
- Beat 2 — COGNITIVE MAPS (our kick for hierarchical / multiscale): the brain does not store a list of facts — it builds a **cognitive map**, a structured representation of how things relate, organized across scales (Behrens 2018), general enough to map even **social** worlds (Son, Bhandari & FeldmanHall 2021 — a cognitive map of a social network). → *our angle:* the object we're after is a multi-scale relational structure, so the trace is structural, not a scalar.
- Beat 3 — OFFLINE CONSOLIDATION (the encoding-vs-inference arc): the inferred order is **not** present right after learning — it is consolidated **offline, at rest** (Ellenbogen 2007) — which forces the distinction between *what you were shown* (encoding) and *what you worked out* (inference). BEHAVIOUR ONLY, and that's fine. → *our angle:* rest is the window; encoding vs inference is the arc.
- Beat 4 — REPLAY (our key for the trace): something is physically happening in that offline window — learned structure is spontaneously **reactivated and reorganized at rest** (Liu, Dolan, Kurth-Nelson & Behrens 2019; founding animal replay: Wilson & McNaughton 1994). → *our angle:* that offline reorganization is exactly the *trace* we look for in the resting network.
- Beat 5 — THE GAP → OUR MOVE (detect → characterize): all four are pinned by behaviour and theory; nobody has *read* that offline, multi-scale reorganization in the brain's **network**, quantitatively. That is the step from **detecting** a change to **characterizing** *what kind* — which scale, which rhythm, focal or distributed. (This is the single anchor figure.)
- Beat 6 — EPILEPSY (concrete coda, not vague): and it is not only cognition. Epilepsy — why these patients are implanted — is itself a **disorder of cortical network organization that spans scales** (Kramer & Cash 2012). So the **same multiscale lens** characterizes the **epileptogenic network** too, in the *same recording*. (Motivation, not a result.)
- Guardrail (repeated all talk): real only if it beats the **boring explanation** — that the change merely sat where connectivity is *strong* (matched-strength null).

4. SPEECH  (~130 s — NAMES each paper as it introduces the anchor, and POINTS at the on-slide figures)
Let me tell you why this matters. Humans are extraordinarily good at something machine learning, for all its data, still isn't: rich inference from very little — exactly the tournament you solved a moment ago. That few-shot relational reasoning is a hallmark of human intelligence, and it's worth knowing how the brain pulls it off.

Three ideas from the literature [gesture the header column, right] tell us where to look. First, cognitive maps. The brain doesn't store a list of facts — Behrens, in "What is a cognitive map?", calls it a structured representation of how things relate, organised across scales. And it's general: Son and FeldmanHall show the same map organises social worlds too [point: the social-network figure, bottom-left] — inferring ties you never saw from the few you did. So the object we're after is a multi-scale relational structure.

Second, offline consolidation. Ellenbogen and Walker, 2007 — the title says it, "human relational memory requires time and sleep": the inferred order isn't there right after learning, it's assembled offline, at rest. That forces the distinction this whole talk turns on — what you were shown, encoding, versus what you worked out, inference.

Third, replay. Liu and Behrens, 2019 — human replay spontaneously reorganises experience, learned structure reactivated and reshuffled at rest [point: the replay schematic, bottom-left]. So there's a resting trace to go and look for.

But all of that was shown through behaviour and theory. Nobody has read that offline, multi-scale reorganisation directly in the brain's network. That's our step — from detecting that something changed to characterising what kind of change [point: detect-vs-characterize, bottom-right]: not one did-it-change number, but a profile across scales — flat, peaked at a characteristic scale, or emerging only when you zoom out.

And it isn't only cognition. Kramer and Cash frame epilepsy as a disorder of cortical network organisation that spans scales — so the same lens reads the epileptogenic network, in the very same recording. One catch I'll repeat all talk: whatever we find counts only if it beats the boring explanation — that the change simply sat where the connections are already strongest.
