# Slide 5 — Higher-order features and the forgotten multiscale

1. TITLE
Higher-order features and the forgotten multiscale

2. MAIN CONCEPT
- Network neuroscience is mature, but basic graph metrics have been mined — the field is hungry for more.
- Two roads beyond pairwise: hypergraphs / simplicial complexes (extra modelling) — and the multiscale, which needs no modelling beyond the pairwise graph.
- The brain is intrinsically multiscale along three axes (Betzel & Bassett 2017): time (rhythms), space (neuron → population → lobe), and topology (single edges → communities → whole-network organization). This topological axis is a coarse-graining scale — the diffusion lens (slide 7) sweeps it; simplices stay on the higher-order road above, not here.
- Any single-scale analysis loses phenomena that live across scales — and cognition is assumed to be exactly that kind of cross-scale emergence.
- Multiscale-from-topology has lacked a simple, controllable lens — that gap is what we fill (and why it matters is the next slide).

3. ON-SLIDE TEXT
beyond pairwise: hypergraphs — or the multiscale (no extra modelling features)

4. SPEECH
Network neuroscience is mature — the simple graph metrics are well mined, and the field wants more. There are two ways past pairwise. One adds structure: hypergraphs, simplicial complexes. The other, quieter one is multiscale — and it needs no extra modelling, just the graph you already have. That matters because the brain is multiscale by nature, along three axes: time through its rhythms, space from neurons to lobes, and topology from pairwise links to collective structure. Cognition is assumed to live across those scales, so a single-scale analysis throws away the phenomenon. What's been missing is a simple, controllable way to read multiscale structure off the topology — and, as the next slide argues, that's not just elegant, it's where the interesting signatures hide.
