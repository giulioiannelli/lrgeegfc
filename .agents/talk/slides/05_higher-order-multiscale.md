# Slide 5 — Multiscale organization: a higher-order feature

1. TITLE
Multiscale organization: a higher-order feature

(Was "Higher-order features and the forgotten multiscale" — retired. Article fixed: "a higher-order",
not "an". The title IS the thesis: multiscale is not a separate road from higher-order — it is one.)

2. MAIN CONCEPT
- **Head (the juice):** the frontier past pairwise connectivity is **higher-order** — and multiscale
  organization is *itself* a higher-order feature. Two roads share that family: an **explicit** one
  (hypergraphs, simplicial complexes) that adds many-body structure by hand and already captures real
  phenomenology, and an **implicit** one (multiscale) that reads collective, beyond-pairwise structure
  straight off the graph you already have — no extra modelling. This slide plants that reframe and the
  three axes; the stakes are the next slide, the lens later in the methods.
- **The three axes — the CORE (right figure, Betzel & Bassett 2017).** The brain is multiscale along
  three axes at once: **time** (rhythms), **space** (neuron → population → lobe), and **topology**
  (single edges → communities → whole-network organization). The **topological axis is a coarse-graining
  scale** — that is the one our diffusion lens (slide 10) will sweep.
- **Explicit higher-order — the well-tooled road we name and set aside.** Hypergraphs / simplicial
  complexes model many-body interactions directly; a rich, active toolbox that captures phenomenology —
  task decoding, individual fingerprinting, brain–behaviour (Santoro et al. 2024). Powerful, but it adds
  modelling machinery on top of the pairwise graph.
- **Implicit higher-order — multiscale, our road.** Coarse-grain the pairwise graph and collective
  structure appears: groupings that no single edge carries. That is a **higher-order *graph* feature**
  (never "nonlinear" — the substrate stays second-order |ImCoh|), obtained with **no extra modelling
  beyond the graph** — exactly what LRG diffusion produces.
- **The gap → our move.** Any single-scale analysis discards what lives *across* scales, and cognition is
  assumed to be precisely that cross-scale emergence. Multiscale-from-topology has lacked a simple,
  controllable lens — that gap is what we fill. (Why it matters = slide 6; the lens = slide 10.)

4. SPEECH  (~65 s — leads with the three-axis figure, then the explicit/implicit split)
The brain is multiscale by nature, organised along three axes at once. [right figure] Time, through its rhythms; space, from single neurons up to whole lobes; and topology, from pairwise links up to communities and whole-network structure. Read it at a single scale and you throw away whatever lives across scales — and cognition is assumed to be exactly that kind of cross-scale emergence.

Now, the frontier past simple pairwise connectivity is higher-order. One road is explicit — hypergraphs, simplicial complexes — you add many-body structure by hand, and it already captures real phenomenology: decoding tasks, fingerprinting individuals, tracking behaviour. But there's a second, quieter higher-order that needs no extra modelling at all: multiscale organisation itself. Coarse-grain the graph you already have and collective, beyond-pairwise structure appears — groupings no single edge carries. That's an implicit higher-order feature of the topology. What's been missing is a simple, controllable way to read it — and that's what we bring.
